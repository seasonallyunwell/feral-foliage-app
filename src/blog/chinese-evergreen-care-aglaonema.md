## Meet the Aglaonema Family

<p>Aglaonema goes by "Chinese Evergreen" — a name that undersells it completely. Yes, it's been a reliable office plant since the Reagan administration. Yes, it tolerates neglect. But the newer varieties? They're genuinely striking — the kind of plant people ask about.</p>

<p>The genus spans dozens of species and hundreds of cultivars. The main groups you'll encounter:</p>

<p><strong>Silver/White varieties</strong> — 'Silver Bay', 'Maria', 'Cutlass'. These are the workhorses of the group — hardy, tolerant, with beautiful silver-green patterning.</p>

<p><strong>Red/Pink varieties</strong> — 'Red Valentine', 'Pink Splash', 'Siam Aurora'. Bolder coloring, sometimes with darker green edges. The pinker the variety, the more light it usually wants to maintain that color.</p>

<p><strong>Green varieties</strong> — The classic Aglaonema modestum and its cousins. The most shade-tolerant of the bunch. If you have a genuinely dark corner, these are your best bet.</p>

<p><strong>Variegated varieties</strong> — 'Crete', 'White Rain', 'Prince's Blood'. High visual drama, but the variegation means less chlorophyll — so they need more light than solid-colored varieties. Don't put a variegated Aglaonema in a corner and expect it to stay colorful.</p>

<p>The variety comparison above shows just how much visual range this group has. If you've been ignoring Aglaonema because you think it's the boring lobby plant, you're overdue for a second look.</p>

## Light: Why Dark Rooms Are Actually Its Happy Place

<p>Here's the thing most "low-light plant" lists get wrong: Pothos will survive in low light. Aglaonema will actually <em>thrive</em> in it.</p>

<p>Aglaonema originates from the forest floors of Southeast Asia — it's evolved to photosynthesize under a dense tree canopy where direct light rarely hits. That means it's efficient in low light conditions that would leave other plants pale and leggy.</p>

<img src="/images/aglaonema-low-light-room.jpg" alt="Healthy Aglaonema Chinese Evergreen thriving in a dark office corner with minimal natural light" style="width:100%;border-radius:10px;margin:16px 0">

<figure>
<figcaption>Low-light proof: this Aglaonema has been thriving in this north-facing office corner for three years with zero direct sunlight.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p><strong>What it tolerates:</strong> Low to medium indirect light. North-facing windows. Fluorescent office lighting. Rooms with sheer curtains that never get direct sun.</p>

<p><strong>What it doesn't tolerate:</strong> Direct sunlight. South or west-facing windows without protection. The kind of bright direct light that would make a human squint will scorch Aglaonema leaves — brown patches, yellow halos, the whole sad affair.</p>

<p><strong>Variegation and light:</strong> If you have a variegated Aglaonema and the colors are fading to green, it's reaching for more light. Move it closer to a window, but still indirect. Solid green varieties can go deeper into a room.</p>

## Water: The Number One Mistake (And How to Avoid It)

<p>If you've killed an Aglaonema, the odds are overwhelming that it was watering-related. Almost always overwatering. Occasionally underwatering (but that's usually fixable before the damage is severe).</p>

<p><strong>How often to water Chinese Evergreen:</strong> Every 1-2 weeks, allowing the top 50% of soil to dry out between waterings. In winter, when growth slows, stretch that to every 2-3 weeks. Always use the finger test — if the soil feels damp two knuckles down, wait.</p>

<p>Aglaonema has a tolerance for drought that most "low-light" plants don't. It stores water in its stems. It'll tell you it's thirsty (slight droop, leaves curling inward) and bounce back when you water it. Overwatering, by contrast, leads to root rot — and by the time you see yellowing lower leaves and mushy stems, the roots are already compromised.</p>

<img src="/images/aglaonema-overwatering-symptoms.jpg" alt="Close-up comparison of overwatered vs healthy Aglaonema leaves showing yellowing and drooping" style="width:100%;border-radius:10px;margin:16px 0">

<figure>
<figcaption>Overwatering red flags: yellowing lower leaves, mushy stems, and soil that stays wet for weeks are the first signs something's off.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p><strong>The finger test:</strong> Stick your index finger into the soil up to the second knuckle. If it's dry at that depth, water. If it's damp, wait. Don't water on a schedule — water based on the plant's actual thirst.</p>

## Soil & Potting: Drainage Is Everything

<p>Aglaonema roots are the demanding type. They want oxygen. They want fast drainage. They do not want to sit in soggy soil for days.</p>

<p>The right mix is a well-draining, chunky aroid blend. Standard potting soil holds too much moisture — your Aglaonema will thank you for adding drainage material.</p>

<p><strong>DIY recipe:</strong> Mix standard peat-based potting soil with perlite (about 1:1 ratio) and a handful of orchid bark or coco coir. The perlite keeps things airy; the bark adds chunkiness that air pockets love.</p>

<img src="/images/aglaonema-soil-repotting.jpg" alt="Well-draining aroid soil mix in a pot with perlite and bark visible, ready for Aglaonema repotting" style="width:100%;border-radius:10px;margin:16px 0">

<figure>
<figcaption>The right mix: chunky perlite, orchid bark, and a peat-based potting medium. This fast-draining blend is exactly what Aglaonema roots want.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p><strong>When to repot:</strong> Every 2-3 years, or when you see roots coming out of the drainage hole. Spring or early summer is ideal. Don't jump up to a dramatically larger pot — go one size up (about 2 inches larger in diameter). Too much soil holds too much water and the roots can't use it.</p>

## Temperature & Humidity: The Secret Killers

<p>Aglaonema is forgiving about a lot of things. Temperature is not one of them — at least, cold temperature isn't.</p>

<p>This plant comes from tropical Asia. It does not know what a cold draft is supposed to feel like. And it will not recover gracefully from one.</p>

<p><strong>Cold sensitivity:</strong> Below 55°F (13°C), Aglaonema starts to show damage. Below 45°F, you're looking at serious tissue death — blackened leaves, mushy stems, the whole grim scenario. If you keep your plant near a window in winter, make sure it's not touching the glass. Window glass gets cold. Cold glass + Aglaonema leaf = damage.</p>

<p><strong>Draughts:</strong> AC vents, doorways, anywhere cold air gusts through — these are Aglaonema killers. If you have a plant near an air conditioning vent or a frequently opened front door, that's your problem. Move the plant, redirect the vent, or accept that this particular corner isn't for Aglaonema.</p>

<p><strong>Temperature range:</strong> 65-80°F (18-27°C) is the happy zone. That's normal room temperature for most homes and offices.</p>

<p><strong>Humidity:</strong> Aglaonema isn't fussy about humidity. Average household humidity is fine. It won't throw a tantrum if the air is dry like some tropicals do. That said, if you live somewhere very dry (desert climates, heated winter air), the occasional misting or a pebble tray won't hurt. It's appreciated, not required.</p>

## Troubleshooting: What's Your Aglaonema Trying to Tell You?

<p>This is where most care guides fall short. They list problems without explaining how to tell them apart. Here's the thing — symptoms overlap. Yellow leaves could be twelve different things. Here's how to narrow it down.</p>

<img src="/images/aglaonema-troubleshooting-diagnostics.jpg" alt="Photo series showing different Aglaonema problems: brown tips, leaf curl, yellow leaf, drooping" style="width:100%;border-radius:10px;margin:16px 0">

<figure>
<figcaption>Know what you're looking at: brown tips (usually water quality), curling (underwatering or humidity), yellow leaves (overwatering), and drooping (thirst or cold shock).</figcaption>
</figure>

<p><strong>Yellow Leaves</strong></p>

<p>Yellowing on an Aglaonema almost always traces back to water — either too much or too little. The key is <em>where</em> the yellowing appears and how fast it spreads.</p>

<p>If yellowing starts on the lower/older leaves and spreads slowly upward — that's normal. Aglaonema regularly retired older leaves as it grows. If the plant looks otherwise healthy and it's been a while since you fertilized, this is just the plant making room for new growth.</p>

<p>If yellowing appears suddenly and affects multiple leaves, especially from the bottom up — that's overwatering. Check the soil. If it's wet two knuckles down after your last watering a week ago, you've got root issues brewing. Let it dry out and don't water again until the soil is genuinely dry. If the yellowing has been going on for a while and the plant looks progressively worse, you may already have root rot — see <a href="/blog/treat-root-rot/">our root rot treatment guide</a>.</p>

<p>If yellowing appears on newer/upper leaves, the plant may be getting too much direct light. Move it back from the window.</p>

<p><strong>Brown Leaf Tips</strong></p>

<p>Brown tips on Aglaonema are most commonly caused by water quality, not watering frequency. Fluoride and chlorine in tap water accumulate and burn the leaf edges — especially in敏感的 (sensitive) varieties. If you're using tap water and getting brown tips despite otherwise good care, try switching to filtered or distilled water for a month and see if the problem stops spreading.</p>

<p>Other causes: low humidity (heater in winter is the usual suspect), over-fertilizing (salt buildup burns roots and shows up in leaves), or underwatering. Check the soil moisture and the recent watering schedule to narrow it down.</p>

<p><strong>Drooping or Limp Leaves</strong></p>

<p>Your Aglaonema is thirsty. The vast majority of the time, drooping means the plant needs water. Water it, and within a few hours the leaves should firm up.</p>

<p>The exceptions: if watering doesn't help, check for cold damage (did a cold draft hit this plant recently?) or root rot (if the soil stays wet and the leaves droop despite moist soil, you may have a rot problem).</p>

<p><strong>Leaf Curling</strong></p>

<p>Leaves curling inward, toward the center of the leaf — this is a stress response. Causes, in order of likelihood:</p>

<p>1. <strong>Underwatering</strong> — the plant is conserving water by curling. Check the soil.</p>

<p>2. <strong>Low humidity</strong> — especially in winter when heating systems dry the air out.</p>

<p>3. <strong>Heat stress</strong> — too close to a radiator, heater vent, or in direct hot airflow.</p>

<p>4. <strong>Pests</strong> — less common but possible. Check the undersides of leaves for spider mites, scale, or mealybugs. Wipe down with neem oil if you find any.</p>

<p><strong>Leggy or Sparse Growth</strong></p>

<p>If your Aglaonema is stretching toward the light — long stems, widely spaced leaves, a generally spindly look — it needs more light. It's not dying, it's desperately reaching. Move it closer to a window. Even a few feet can make a difference. Pruning leggy stems also encourages bushier growth.</p>

<p>For symptoms not covered here, <a href="/blog/overwatering-underwatering/">check our overwatering vs. underwatering guide</a> — most Aglaonema problems fit into that framework.</p>

## Feeding: Keep It Light

<p>Aglaonema is not a heavy feeder. In fact, over-fertilizing is more common than under-fertilizing, and the symptoms look the same (brown tips, salt buildup on soil surface, wilting despite moist soil).</p>

<p><strong>When to fertilize:</strong> During the growing season (spring and summer). Once a month is plenty. Use a standard liquid houseplant fertilizer diluted to half-strength.</p>

<p><strong>When to stop:</strong> Fall and winter. Growth slows and the plant can't use the extra nutrients — they just accumulate in the soil and cause salt damage.</p>

<p><strong>Signs of over-fertilizing:</strong> Brown leaf tips (same as water quality issues — check your watering history), white crust on the soil surface (salt deposits), wilting despite regular watering. If this happens, flush the soil thoroughly with water — run water through the pot multiple times to leech out the buildup — and hold off on fertilizer for a few months.</p>

## Propagation: Easy Wins

<p>Aglaonema propagates two ways: stem cuttings in water, or division when you repot. Both are straightforward.</p>

<p><strong>Stem Cuttings (Water Method)</strong></p>

<p>Find a healthy stem with at least 2-3 inches of length. Cut it below a node (the small bump where a leaf attaches — that's where roots will emerge). Remove the lower leaves so you have a clean stem. Place in a jar of water — clean, room-temperature water. Change it weekly.</p>

<img src="/images/aglaonema-propagation-stem-cutting.jpg" alt="Aglaonema stem cutting in glass jar of water showing root development after 4 weeks" style="width:100%;border-radius:10px;margin:16px 0">

<figure>
<figcaption>Easy propagation: a single stem cutting in water develops roots in 4-6 weeks. Change the water weekly and wait for 2-3 inch roots before potting.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>Expect to see roots in 4-6 weeks. Once the roots are 2-3 inches long, pot in well-draining aroid mix. Don't wait too long — water roots adapt poorly to soil if they get too long and tangled.</p>

<p><strong>Division (At Repotting)</strong></p>

<p>When you repot every 2-3 years, you can separate the root ball into 2-3 plants if the plant has multiple stems. Gently tease the roots apart — they don't need to be cut if you can avoid it. Pot each division in fresh soil at the same depth it was growing before. Water well, then let it settle in.</p>

<p>Division is faster than stem cuttings and less risky — the plant is already established. But it requires a plant large enough to have multiple stems worth separating.</p>

## Toxicity Heads-Up

<p>Aglaonema is toxic to cats and dogs. The calcium oxalate crystals cause oral irritation, drooling, vomiting, and difficulty swallowing if ingested. It's not fatal in most cases, but it's genuinely unpleasant for the animal.</p>

<p>Keep Aglaonema out of reach of pets who chew on plants, and out of reach of small children who explore with their mouths. If you suspect ingestion, call your vet or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435).</p>

<p>This matters. Every year otherwise excellent plant parents learn this the hard way. Don't let it be you.</p>
